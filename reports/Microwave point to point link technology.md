# Microwave links trade reach for capacity

Point-to-point microwave radios carry data between two fixed antennas over a clear line of sight, and the frequency band decides most of the trade-off. Bands below about 11 GHz reach tens of kilometres at modest capacity, while E-band (71–86 GHz) carries **multi-gigabit traffic over hops of roughly 1 to 7 km**. Above about 10–17 GHz, **rain fade rather than distance sets the longest usable hop**, so high-capacity links depend on short hops, adaptive modulation and a chosen availability target. Licensing is shifting as well: US and UK rules for the upper microwave and E-band ranges are moving from case-by-case coordination toward registration and database models, although the final 2026 rules were not confirmed. On cost, **microwave is usually far cheaper than newly trenched fibre**, but where fibre already reaches a site the economics favour fibre, and no independent 2025–2026 per-link cost figure was found. Ericsson's outlook projects a **49% microwave and 51% fibre** split of mobile backhaul by 2030, a forecast that keeps microwave a large share rather than a niche. W- and D-band radios are expected in commercial use only around **2028–2030**, so E-band remains the practical capacity path for current deployments.

## Band choice trades hop length against bandwidth

Line-of-sight planning starts with the first Fresnel zone. The common rule is that **at least 60% of the first Fresnel zone** must be clear, with about 80% preferred. ITU-R P.530 is the formal basis for the 60% rule ([ITU-R P.530-18](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.530-18-202109-I!!PDF-E.pdf), read from a search summary), and Cambium's community guidance is stricter, allowing at most 40% obstruction and recommending 20% or less ([Cambium Networks](https://community.cambiumnetworks.com/t/fresnel-zone-recommendations/54685), snippet). The first-zone radius is r1 = 17.3 × √(d1·d2 / (f·D)) metres, with distances in km and frequency in GHz ([L-com calculator](https://www.l-com.com/resources/wireless-calculators/fresnel-zone-clearance-calculator)). Because the radius shrinks with frequency, the first zone at 80 GHz is about **3.6 times narrower** than at 6 GHz over the same path. High-band links therefore tolerate partial obstruction better than their low-band counterparts, but they demand tighter pointing and alignment.

Hop length follows band. ITU-R F.2086 groups the bands qualitatively: below about 11 GHz for long hops, 11–23 GHz for medium hops, and above 23 GHz for short links ([ITU-R F.2086](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/f/R-REC-F.2086-0-201509-I%21%21PDF-E.pdf)). Secondary sources put typical terrestrial backbone hops at **10 to 60 km** ([Wikipedia, Microwave link](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave_link)). Modelled limits are more specific. For a BPSK link at 186 Mbps with 99.999% rain availability, CommScope's modelling gives **11.31 km at 23 GHz** and **2.44 km at 80 GHz** ([CommScope dual-band paper](https://COMMSCOPE.COM/globalassets/digizuite/602438-dual-band-wp-114222-en.pdf)). These are modelling outputs, not datasheet specifications. Ericsson's 2019 review of a decade of E-band notes that E-band hops were often assumed to be limited to 1–2 km, yet it documents a 7 km hop in rain zone E and reports that **11% of Polish E-band hops exceed 6 km** ([Ericsson, 2019](https://www.ericsson.com/4aef65/assets/local/reports-papers/microwave-outlook/2019/lessons-learned-from-a-decade-with-e-band-article-co-screen.pdf)). No verified band-by-band hop table for the 6–38 GHz range was retrieved.

Capacity is roughly channel bandwidth multiplied by spectral efficiency, and the band sets how much bandwidth is available. E-band holds **10 GHz of spectrum** in two 5 GHz blocks, 71–76 GHz and 81–86 GHz ([Analog Devices](https://www.analog.com/en/resources/technical-articles/e-band-wireless-radio-links.html), snippet). Huawei's RTN 380AX E-band radio lists channels from **62.5 MHz to 2 GHz**, up to **10 Gbps** aggregate, and modulation from BPSK to 1024QAM ([Huawei](https://e.huawei.com/ph/products/wireless/microwave/e-band-millimeter), snippet). Higher-order modulation needs more link margin, so peak rates appear mainly on short or clear-air hops. Ericsson's long-haul material shows the trade directly: the same link can run **1024QAM at 99.99% availability** or **128QAM at 99.999%** ([Ericsson, 2020](https://www.ericsson.com/4a8f9a/assets/local/reports-papers/microwave-outlook/2020/microwave-report-2020-long-haul-use.pdf), snippet). Capacity and availability are a single budget, not two separate specifications.

## Rain fade, not distance, caps high-band reach

At 80 GHz, rain attenuation is about **10 dB/km at 25 mm/h and about 30 dB/km at 100 mm/h** ([CommScope blog](https://www.commscope.com/Blog/Optical-Fiber-in-the-Air), search summary). That threefold swing for the same path explains why the rain zone matters more than frequency alone at E-band. ITU-R F.1102 states that above about 17 GHz, hop length is limited by rain attenuation, and that precipitation outage critically determines it ([ITU-R F.1102](https://itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/f/R-REC-F.1102-1-200202-S!!PDF-E.pdf)). The current prediction method, ITU-R P.530-19 (September 2025), covers rain and multipath effects on digital line-of-sight links and includes gaseous absorption above about 10 GHz ([ITU-R P.530-19 summary](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.530-19-202509-I!!SUM-HTM-E.htm)). The specific-attenuation coefficients needed to compute rain loss at 6–38 GHz were not retrieved, so the lower bands remain unquantified here.

Availability sets the design target, and the arithmetic is unforgiving. **99.999% availability allows about 5.3 minutes of outage per year**. ITU-R F.1703 sets availability objectives for real digital fixed links, but on a 27,500 km hypothetical reference path, so a per-hop target has to be derived from it ([ITU-R F.1703](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/f/R-REC-F.1703-0-200501-I!!PDF-E.pdf)). Relaxing the target is the strongest lever for longer E-band hops ([Ericsson, 2019](https://www.ericsson.com/4aef65/assets/local/reports-papers/microwave-outlook/2019/lessons-learned-from-a-decade-with-e-band-article-co-screen.pdf)). One search summary reports that current 70/80 GHz equipment reaches **1 Gbps at 99.999% weather availability** ([Cablefree](https://cablefree.net/wirelesstechnology/millimeter-wave-technology/e-band-mmw-technology/)). That figure is snippet-only and the author is unidentified, but it shows that the sustained rate in rain-prone areas sits far below the multi-gigabit peak.

The main mitigations are dual-band pairing, adaptive modulation and shorter hops. CommScope's 23/80 GHz and 18/80 GHz antenna work reports that a lower-band companion extends link length and reduces degradation at 80 GHz ([CommScope dual-band paper](https://COMMSCOPE.COM/globalassets/digizuite/602438-dual-band-wp-114222-en.pdf), snippet). Adaptive modulation steps the link down during a fade, which keeps it up at lower capacity, but operators must define service levels to benefit from it ([Microwave Journal](https://www.microwavejournal.com/articles/12354-network-engineering-with-adaptive-modulation)).

## Licences are moving from coordination to registration

Under the US Part 101 framework, which consolidated fixed microwave rules in 1996 ([Federal Register, 1996](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1996-05-28/html/96-12942.htm)), applicants generally must **coordinate proposed frequency use with existing users before filing** ([47 CFR 101.103](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/101.103)). The rule that surprises most readers is that US E-band is not unlicensed. Search summaries describe a non-exclusive nationwide licence combined with registration of each link in a Link Registration System run by third-party database managers, and a link may not operate until it is registered ([Federal Register, 2024](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-04-29/html/2024-05390.htm)). Neither that rule nor its amendment was read in full. Unlicensed millimetre-wave use in the US more likely refers to 60 GHz under Part 15, but no source on that was retrieved.

The 2025 rulemaking points toward databases. The FCC's Upper Microwave NPRM (SB Docket 25-305, FCC 25-70, adopted 28 October 2025) proposes replacing the sharing rule in 47 CFR 25.136 with new sharing paradigms ([FCC 25-70](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-25-70A1.pdf)). One search summary says the proposal evaluates light-licensing with automated registration to replace manual coordination, which has not been confirmed in full text. Commenters on the NPRM proposed a unified system covering the Q/V-, E- and W-bands, and one urged unlicensed or low-power sharing ([Broadband Breakfast](https://broadbandbreakfast.com/fcc-plans-to-overhaul-rules-governing-upper-microwave-spectrum.md), secondary). The outcome was not confirmed.

The UK takes a hybrid route. Ofcom offers **73.375–75.875 GHz and 83.375–85.875 GHz** under a light-licensed, self-coordinated process for point-to-point links, while 10, 28, 32 and 40 GHz are block-assigned ([Ofcom, fixed terrestrial links](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/spectrum/radio-equipment/fixed-terrestrial-links), search summary). In Europe, CEPT's ECC/REC(05)07 sets the 71–76 and 81–86 GHz channel arrangements ([CEPT document 1853](https://docdb.cept.org/download/1853)), and the recommendation for 40.5–43.5 GHz was last amended on **24 January 2025** ([CEPT document 4608](https://docdb.cept.org/download/4608)). The ETSI survey of worldwide E-band regulation dates from 2020 and is useful only as history ([ETSI WP 37](https://www.etsi.org/images/files/ETSIWhitePapers/etsi-WP-37-E-Band-survey-on-Status-of-Worldwide-Regulation.pdf)). The direction of travel is clear even though the final rules are not: manual, case-by-case coordination is giving way to registration. That lowers coordination friction but makes the registry the new point of control. Per-link licence fees and coordination timelines for both Ofcom and the FCC were not found.

## Microwave beats new trenches on cost, not owned fibre

The equipment figures are dated and mostly American. NTIA's cost sheet puts a microwave antenna device at about **US$45,000–55,000 per tower**, excluding foundation, land and tower construction, and notes that microwave networks still need fibre backhaul ([NTIA, Costs at a Glance](https://broadbandusa.ntia.doc.gov/sites/default/files/resource-files/bbusa_costs_at_glance_networks.pdf), undated). Recurring cost matters more over a multi-year horizon. A 2014 RCR Wireless estimate prices tower space at **US$400 per month plus US$100 per linear foot of antenna**, so a link between two 4-foot antennas costs about **US$1,600 per month**, or roughly US$19,000 a year ([RCR Wireless, 2014](https://rcrwireless.com/20140513/fundamentals/microwave-link-design)). A 2024 analyst note says microwave still costs less than wired backhaul, but operators are increasingly concerned about tower rent as operating expense ([RCR Wireless, 2024](https://www.rcrwireless.com/20240903/5g/microwave-technology-continues-to-evolve-to-meet-the-challenges-of-5g-and-ai-data-requests-analyst-angle)). No current tower-lease rate card was found.

Fibre is the comparison that drives the decision, and the benchmarks come from vendors. Ceragon, a microwave supplier, cites a 2025 US fibre-cost survey with medians of **US$18 per foot underground and US$8 per foot aerial**, which works out to about **US$475,000** for a five-mile underground route and **US$211,000** aerial ([Ceragon](https://www.ceragon.com/blog/fiber-vs-wireless-the-greatest-debate-of-the-decade)). Bridgewave's promotional figure of US$24.50 per foot, about US$130,000 per mile, is in the same range ([Bridgewave](https://bridgewave.com/?p=2222)). Ericsson's 2026 messaging makes the same point from the operator side: where fibre is not already at the cell site, deployment cost can be significantly higher than a microwave link ([Ericsson blog, 2026](https://www.ericsson.com/en/blog/middle-east-africa/2026/microwave-is-the-new-fiber-rethinking-5g-backhaul), summary). The inference is that microwave's cost advantage is greatest for new routes over distance and shrinks where fibre exists.

Satellite fares worse on bandwidth economics. Analysys Mason's 2022 model finds the sample case's NPV turns negative once bandwidth costs exceed **US$65 per Mbit/s per month**, though the authors flag their US$8,000-per-km fibre assumption as possibly misleading ([Analysys Mason, 2022](https://analysysmason.com/about-us/news/newsletter/backhaul-satellite-fibre-quarterly/article-pdf)). A GSMA-cited figure puts satellite at about 1.9% of backhaul links, but the primary source was not confirmed ([GSMA, 2019](https://www.gsma.com/spectrum/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Mobile-Backhaul-Options.pdf)). On latency, Ookla's Q1 2025 data gives a median of **45 ms for Starlink against about 683–684 ms for GEO services** ([ComSoc TechBlog](https://techblog.comsoc.org/tag/hughesnet-and-viasat/), secondary). Starlink's own network update reports a median peak-hour latency of **25.7 ms** in June 2025 ([Starlink](https://starlink.com/updates/network-update)). Fibre remains the lowest-latency option in these sources, but no microwave latency figure was found, so the microwave comparison stays unquantified.

Free-space optics competes on the same routes but fails differently. A six-day trial of a **4.6 km urban coherent FSO link**, which ran from clear sky through thick fog, achieved **92% availability including slow fades and 99% excluding them**, at 500 Gb/s ([arXiv 2507.06051](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.06051)). The gap shows that prolonged fog, not brief fades, drives the outages. ABI Research recommends pairing FSO with microwave as the adverse-weather fallback ([ABI Research](https://www.abiresearch.com/market-research/insight/7784759-with-advancements-in-technical-capabilitie)), and ITU-R P.1814-1 (September 2025) gives the prediction methods for FSO attenuation in clear air, fog and rain ([ITU-R P.1814-1](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.1814-1-202509-I!!PDF-E.pdf)).

## E-band radios extend reach while W and D bands wait

The near-term product story is E-band. Ceragon launched the IP-50EXP on **18 June 2025**, a high-power E-band radio with 24 dBm extended transmit power, low receive thresholds and an optional E-stabilizer antenna, aimed at link distances comparable to traditional microwave ([Ceragon](https://www.ceragon.com/news/ceragon-introduces-ip-50exp-delivering-millimeter-wave-capacity-over-microwave-distances)). Ceragon also reports three E-band proofs of concept with Tier-1 operators and an ISP in North America and Europe, though the dates were not confirmed ([Barchart, Ceragon release](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/35603438/ceragon-demonstrates-e-band-innovations-in-tier-1-and-isp-deployments-across-north-america-and-europe)). Those are company claims. V-band at 60 GHz has been slower to grow: Ericsson's 2022 report noted that first-generation V-band products never reached volume ([Ericsson, 2022](https://www.ericsson.com/4a81b8/assets/local/reports-papers/microwave-outlook/2022/microwave-report-2022-spectrum-in-a-dynamic-market.pdf), historical).

W- and D-band are the next capacity tier, and their reach is the open question. Ericsson's 2025 outlook says the W-band achieves about **90% of E-band hop lengths** and the D-band about **60%** ([Ericsson Microwave Outlook 2025](https://www.ericsson.com/4a8a43/assets/local/reports-papers/microwave-outlook/2025/microwave-outlook-2025_report.pdf), summary). A W-band trial with Ericsson, Deutsche Telekom and Cosmote reported **5.7 Gbps over 1.5 km** and more than 10 Gbps over 1 km, with the trial date unconfirmed ([ABI Research](https://abiresearch.com/market-research/product/7779446-5g-backhaul-dipping-toes-in-w-band-frequen)). Nokia's dual-polarisation D-band work projects **25 Gbps per direction on a single 2 GHz channel** and first commercial deployments between 2028 and 2030 ([Nokia](https://www.nokia.com/blog/innovation-for-d-band-microwave-transport-dual-polar-capacity-boost/), vendor projection). Spectrum is ample: ABI puts roughly 15 GHz in the W-band and 30 GHz in the D-band.

Two operational developments round out the picture. Ericsson's 2026 outlook discusses satellite interference with fixed E-band links and calls it manageable ([Ericsson Microwave Outlook 2026](https://www.ericsson.com/4a9c7f/assets/local/reports-papers/microwave-outlook/2026/microwave-outlook-report-2026.pdf), summary). Ericsson's 2025 AI report describes O2 Telefónica Germany using AI to detect slight antenna misalignment after storms and to locate developing cable faults, a case the operator said kept a site from becoming a "Red Site" ([Ericsson Microwave Outlook 2025, AI-based backhaul management](https://www.ericsson.com/4a8a42/assets/local/reports-papers/microwave-outlook/2025/microwave-outlook-2025_report_ai-based-backhaul-management.pdf), vendor-reported and not independently verified). The same report cites an outage caused by a construction crane blocking a link during working hours. The market picture is mixed. Dell'Oro says global point-to-point microwave equipment revenue **fell 1% in 2025**, with weak second-half demand in India and Latin America, even as mobile backhaul shipments rose, and the top six vendors' share rose to **87%** ([Microwave Journal](https://www.microwavejournal.com/articles/45406-microwave-transmission-equipment-market-flattens-out-in-2025), summary). Dell'Oro's forecasts disagree on timing: a February 2026 release expects growth through about 2027, while a later report has fibre overtaking wireless with a peak around 2028 ([Dell'Oro, February 2026](https://www.delloro.com/?p=6492), summary). Ceragon, for its part, narrowed full-year 2026 revenue guidance to **US$365–375 million** in November 2026 and cited growing E-band interest ([Ceragon Q3 2026](https://finviz.com/news/416707/ceragon-reports-2026-third-quarter-financial-results)).

## Conclusion

Read together, these sources show that the binding constraint on microwave is moving from spectrum toward availability and regulatory access. E-band's multi-gigabit peaks are real, but the sustained rate depends on rain zone and availability target as much as on the radio. An operator who relaxes 99.999% to 99.99% can lengthen hops; one who holds the target has to shorten them and buy margin. Band selection is therefore a planning decision built on rain statistics, not a product choice.

The regulatory shift points the same way. Registration models reduce manual coordination, but they turn the link database into the point of control, and the 2025 US and UK changes remain unconfirmed. The cost case is most sensitive to recurring tower rent, which no current source quantifies. Planners should price tower leases and fix the availability target before choosing a band, and they should treat vendor cost claims as marketing until checked against current quotes.
